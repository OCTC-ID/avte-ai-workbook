Over the last few weeks you checked AI’s answers before trusting them, held some things back, and decided where AI fits in your course. People who study this call it AI literacy. It means knowing enough about these tools, and having the right habits, to use them well and to help others do the same.

The SEE Framework

One useful way to think about AI literacy comes from AI for Education, a group that trains teachers across the country. They call it the SEE Framework. It describes AI literacy as using generative AI safely, ethically, and effectively, and it says those three always work together.

  • Safely is about protecting yourself and others. That covers privacy, and it also covers keeping your own thinking and judgment in charge.
  • Ethically is about how your use affects other people. That includes being honest about when you used AI and following the guidelines where you work or study.
  • Effectively is about getting real value from the tool. You give it clear direction, check what comes back, and keep working with it until the result is useful.

Underneath all three, the framework puts two things. One is a basic understanding of how these tools work, like what you read in Chapter 1. The other is a set of habits of mind, such as staying skeptical of answers that sound sure and being open about your use.

The framework’s point is that one of these on its own isn’t enough. Someone can protect their privacy perfectly and still use AI in a way that adds nothing to their learning. Someone else can get great results while sharing information they shouldn’t. You need all three.

You’ve already been doing this

Here’s where the workbook lined up with SEE:

  • In Chapter 1 you learned how these tools produce answers, and what to keep out of them.
  • In Chapter 2, the moment you paused before pasting something in was a safety call.
  • In Chapters 3 and 4 you checked AI’s work with your clinical eye and your teaching eye. That’s effective use, and it’s also the habit of staying skeptical.
  • In Chapter 6 you wrote an expectation that asks students to be open about their use. That’s the ethical part.

The assignment side has names too. You met one of them on the TILT page in Part 1. The other is the idea behind Chapter 5, making one meaningful change instead of rebuilding a course. James Lang calls that small teaching, and his book of the same name is full of small changes like the one you made.
