AI and Your Teaching

Part 3 · Back in your course · Chapter 12

Try these with your mentor group or class

Each activity takes 10 to 20 minutes

Works toward · Create

Design an AI-informed teaching approach and a clear course AI-use expectation for your context.

This chapter helps you carry your teaching approach into your course by building AI literacy with colleagues or students.

You’ll leave with

Short activities you can run with your mentor group or your students, ready to print, with vet tech examples already written.

Each of these works with colleagues or with students. They’re short enough for the start of a class or a mentor group meeting. Use the examples as they are, or swap in your own.

The formats are adapted from the activity bank in the SEE Framework guide (see Chapter 8). The vet tech examples are written for this workbook.

What would you share?

10 to 15 minutes · works best in pairs or small groups

People sort information into what they would put into an AI tool, what they wouldn’t, and what depends on the situation.

  1. Give each pair or group the list below.
  2. Have them sort each item into Would share, Wouldn’t share, or It depends.
  3. Compare sorts across groups. Spend the most time on the items people disagreed about.
  4. Close by connecting the discussion to your program’s privacy rules and your clinical sites’ policies.

Items to sort

  • A client’s name and phone number
  • A radiograph with the clinic and patient names removed
  • Your clinical site’s standard operating procedures
  • A classmate’s care plan
  • A drug calculation problem from your textbook
  • A photo of a patient chart
  • Your own lecture notes
  • A description of a patient with no identifying details

Adapted from “What Should You Share?” in AI for Education’s SEE Framework guide (2026).

Where does AI do well?

15 to 20 minutes · needs at least one device with an AI tool

People predict which tasks AI will handle well and which it will get wrong, then test a few. AI is strong at some tasks and weak at others that look just as easy. Researchers call this a jagged frontier.

  1. Show the task list below. Have everyone mark each task as “AI will do well” or “AI will struggle.”
  2. Test two or three of the tasks live with an AI tool.
  3. Check the results with your clinical eye. Were the predictions right?
  4. Talk about where each person will use AI, and where they won’t, based on what they saw.

Tasks

  • Write a client discharge handout for a dog going home after a dental cleaning
  • Calculate a constant rate infusion from a given dose and fluid rate
  • Identify a parasite egg from a photo
  • Explain the stages of anesthesia in plain language
  • Build a study schedule for the VTNE
  • Decide whether a patient in a case description is stable for anesthesia

Adapted from “Jagged Frontier mapping” in AI for Education’s SEE Framework guide (2026). The term comes from Dell’Acqua, F., et al. (2023), Navigating the jagged technological frontier, Harvard Business School Working Paper 24-013.

When is it OK?

10 to 15 minutes · individual, then small groups

People rate how comfortable they are with different uses of AI, then compare their ratings and talk through the differences.

  1. Read each scenario below. Have everyone rate it from 1 (not OK) to 5 (completely fine).
  2. In small groups, compare ratings. Look for the scenarios where people disagreed most.
  3. Ask what would need to change to make an uncomfortable scenario OK.
  4. With students, connect the discussion to your course expectation.

Scenarios

  • A student uses AI to quiz themselves before a pharmacology exam
  • An instructor uses AI to draft the first version of a case study, then checks it
  • A student uses AI to write a SOAP note for a real patient at their externship site
  • An instructor uses AI to write feedback on student care plans
  • A student uses AI to reword a discussion post so it sounds more polished
  • A student who struggles with English uses AI to check the grammar of a lab report

Adapted from “When Is It OK?” in AI for Education’s SEE Framework guide (2026).

Keep going, one change at a time

You don’t have to redesign your whole course to make it better. This fall you made one change to one assignment, wrote one clear expectation, and maybe tried one activity. ThatThat’s the pace that lasts.rsquo;s a pace you can keep up.

James Lang calls this approach small teaching. A change doesn’t have to be big or time-consuming to help students learn. It has to be deliberate, and it has to be something you’ll actually keep doing. Small also doesn’t mean lower standards. It means improvement you can sustain while you’re teaching a full load.

When you’re ready for the next change, pick one thing, try it, see how it goes, and build from there. If you want company along the way, the Keep learning page lists the books and resources behind this workbook, starting with Lang’s.
