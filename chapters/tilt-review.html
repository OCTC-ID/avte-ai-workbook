A quick review of TILT

About 7 minutes

You’ll leave with

Three questions you can ask about any assignment, ready to use when you revise your test or assignment on December 1.

What TILT is

TILT stands for Transparency in Learning and Teaching. It was developed by Mary-Ann Winkelmes, who founded the TILT Higher Ed project. The idea is to tell students plainly why they’re doing an assignment, what exactly to do, and how their work will be judged.

Most of us already know these things about our own assignments. They just don’t always make it onto the page.

Why it’s worth the effort

In a large multi-campus study, students whose courses used more transparent assignments reported more academic confidence, a stronger sense of belonging, and more awareness of the skills they were building. The gains were larger for first-generation, low-income, and underrepresented students. That describes a lot of vet tech students.

The change itself is small. You add what’s missing to an assignment you already have.

The three parts

TILT calls itThe plain questionWhat it sounds like for students
Purpose Why does this matter? What they’ll learn, in words they recognize, and where they’ll use it after this course: on the job, with clients, or on the VTNE.
Task What exactly do I do? The steps in a sensible order, clear action words (list, compare, explain, demonstrate), common mistakes to avoid, and what to turn in.
Criteria What does good work look like? How excellent work differs from work that’s just okay, shared ahead of time as a checklist, rubric, or example. Then students can judge their own work while they’re doing it.

A before and after

Before

Write a client handout on home care after a dental cleaning. Due Friday. 20 points.

After

Why it matters: In practice, you’ll send clients home with instructions they read when they’re tired and worried. Clear written instructions prevent callbacks and complications. This assignment builds that skill.

What to do:

  1. Pick a patient from this week’s lab or rotation and note what made their case different.
  2. Draft a one-page handout for that patient’s owner: feeding, pain signs, when to call.
  3. Trade drafts with a classmate and mark anything a client might misread.
  4. Revise, then turn in your final handout and two sentences on what you changed and why.

What good looks like: Plain language a client can follow, instructions that fit your specific patient, and clear signs for when to call. The rubric is attached, along with a strong example from last term.

Where AI comes in

Each part also changes what AI can do with the assignment.

  • Purpose: when students see why the skill matters to their future work, handing it to a tool is less tempting.
  • Task: steps like a real patient, a peer swap, and a note on what changed are hard to hand off. AI can draft a generic handout. It can’t know what made your lab patient different.
  • Criteria: when the rubric rewards fit to a real case and the student’s own reasoning, a polished but generic answer earns less.

We’ll use these three questions on December 1 when you revise the test or assignment you tried in Chapter 3. You’ll pick just one to change.

Try it

Optional, and quick. Look at the test or assignment you used in Chapter 3 exactly as it’s written now. Jot what’s already on the page for each part, even if it’s “nothing yet.”

Does the assignment tell students what they’ll learn and where they’ll use it?
Are the steps clear and in order? Does it say what to avoid and what to turn in?
How will students know they’ve done well? Do they see the rubric or an example before they start?
Which part is thinnest on the page right now?

Hold onto this. On December 1 you’ll pick one part to strengthen.

You’ll know you’re ready when

You’ve looked at your assignment through all three questions and you know which part is thinnest.
