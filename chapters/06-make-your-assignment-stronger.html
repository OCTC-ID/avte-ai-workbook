AI and Your Teaching

Part 2 · When we meet · Chapter 6

Make your assignment stronger

About 25 minutes, during the session

Works toward · Create

Design an AI-informed teaching approach and a clear course AI-use expectation for your context.

This chapter is your AI-informed teaching approach. You make one change to your test or assignment, based on what AI did with it, and then you check whether it worked.

You’ll leave with

One change to your test or assignment that you’ll make before January, written the way your students will see it. It goes on your Week 7 takeaway.

What AI’s result is telling you

If AI passed your test, the test is probably asking for something AI does well: recalling facts, summarizing, or writing a polished answer. The content may be exactly right. The test just isn’t showing yet what your students can do on their own.

If AI struggled, look at where. That part of your test may already be asking students to show real skill, and you may want more of it.

One change is enough

You don’t need to rebuild anything. Pick the part where a small change would make the biggest difference, and make that change well. One good change before January will do more for your students than a plan to redo the whole course.

This idea has a name. James Lang calls it small teaching: small, focused changes, grounded in research on how people learn, that you make in the course you already teach. Small changes are easier to make well, easier to keep, and easier to learn from.

Where to look

These are the three questions from Chapter 4, in plain words.

  • Why it matters (TILT: Purpose). Can students see why doing this themselves matters for the work they’ll do? For example, connect a dosage question to the moment a tech double-checks an order before drawing it up.
  • What to do (TILT: Task). Do the steps ask for something AI can’t do for them? For example, use a case from your own lab, ask for a draft before the final, or have students talk you through their reasoning in a short check-in.
  • What good looks like (TILT: Criteria). Does your rubric give credit for reasoning as well as for the final answer? For example, add a row for explaining why they chose that step.
In your breakout

Each person names their one change out loud. Then your group picks one to share with everyone when we come back.
