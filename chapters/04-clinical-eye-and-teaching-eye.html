Part 2 · When we meet · Chapter 4

Your clinical eye and your teaching eye

About 25 minutes, during the session

Works toward · Evaluate

Evaluate the benefits, risks, and ethics of generative AI in teaching and assessment.

In this chapter you put your judgment in writing. You look at what AI did with your test, then name one benefit, one risk, and one ethical line for your own teaching.

You’ll leave with

Your own judgment of AI, in writing. You’ll check what AI did with your test using both kinds of judgment you bring to this job. Then you’ll write one sentence each about a benefit, a risk, and an ethical line for your teaching. Those sentences go on your Week 7 takeaway.

You already have a clinical eye. It’s what catches the wrong dose before anyone else does. This fall you’ve been building a teaching eye, the one that notices when a quiz doesn’t match what students are supposed to learn. Anything AI gives you deserves a look from both.

Your clinical eye

Your clinical eye asks whether something is right and safe. Is the dose in range? Are the steps in the right order? Would this work on a real patient in a real clinic? You’ve used that judgment for years. It still works when the answer comes from AI, and AI needs it. An AI answer can sound completely sure and still be wrong in a way only a clinician would catch.

Your teaching eye

Your teaching eye asks something different. Does this test show what a student can actually do? It notices when a quiz rewards looking something up. It notices when two instructors would score the same answer two different ways. You’ve been building it since October.

A test can be clinically accurate and still easy for AI to pass. That’s a teaching-eye problem, and it’s the one Chapter 5 helps you fix.

What we’ll do together

In the session you’ll share one catch in the chat, add your result to the group scoreboard, and watch the teaching-eye questions used on a real test. Fill this chapter in as we go, or right after.

Step 2

Look with your clinical eye
