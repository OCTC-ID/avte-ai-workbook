AI and Your Teaching

Part 1 · Before we meet · Chapter 1

What AI tools can and can’t do

About 5 minutes

Works toward · Evaluate

Evaluate the benefits, risks, and ethics of generative AI in teaching and assessment.

This chapter gives you a starting picture of where AI helps and where it slips, and of what to keep out of AI tools.

You’ll leave with

A plain picture of what’s happening when you type into an AI tool, and a short list of what to keep out of the tool.

Some of you use these tools every day, and some of you have never opened one. Either way, you’re in the right place. This chapter gets everyone to the same starting line, so on December 1 we can spend our time on your own courses instead of the basics of AI.

What’s actually happening

Tools like ChatGPT, Copilot, Gemini, and Claude learned from a huge amount of writing. When you type a request, the tool builds its response one piece at a time, based on what usually comes next in similar writing. Because the answers come from patterns in real writing, they sound natural, almost like talking with someone who knows the subject.

That smooth writing is also the reason to stay alert. A confident answer can still be wrong, and the tool sounds just as sure when it’s wrong as when it’s right. Deciding whether an answer is correct is still your job, and your clinical training has already prepared you for exactly that kind of checking.

Where it helps

Used well, AI can take some of the busywork off your plate. It’s good at:

  • Getting a first draft on the page: an email, a handout, a set of practice questions
  • Rewording something for a different reader, like a client or a first-semester student
  • Brainstorming when you’re stuck
  • Explaining a topic at a few different levels

Where it slips

AI also has a few habits to watch for:

  • It states wrong facts with full confidence, including doses, ranges, and procedures.
  • It sometimes invents sources, quotes, or references that don’t exist.
  • It doesn’t know your program, your clinic, your students, or your state’s rules unless you tell it.
  • AI doesn’t always save time, because checking its answers takes time too. Your expertise is what makes the checking quick and accurate.

What stays out

Treat anything you type into a free AI tool like something you’d write on a whiteboard in a busy hallway. Keep these out:

  • Client names, contact details, or account information
  • Patient records that could identify a client or practice
  • Student names, grades, or student work
  • Anything your institution or clinic treats as confidential

Your institution may also have its own rules about which tools you can use, so ask if you’re not sure. And if you ever find yourself hesitating before you paste something in, listen to that hesitation. We’ll come back to it on December 1.
