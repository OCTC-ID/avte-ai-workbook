Part 1 · Before we meet · Chapter 1

What AI tools can and can’t do

About 5 minutes

You’ll leave with

A plain picture of what’s happening when you type into an AI tool, and a short list of what to keep out of it.

Some of you use these tools every day. Some of you have never opened one. Both are completely fine for this week. This chapter gets everyone to the same starting line.

What’s actually happening

Tools like ChatGPT, Copilot, Gemini, and Claude learned from a huge amount of writing. When you type a request, the tool writes a response one piece at a time, based on what usually comes next in writing like it. That’s why it sounds so fluent.

It also explains the most important thing to remember: sounding right and being right are two different things. The tool can be wrong in the same confident, polished voice it uses when it’s right.

Where it helps

  • Getting a first draft on the page: an email, a handout, a set of practice questions
  • Rewording something for a different reader, like a client or a first-semester student
  • Brainstorming when you’re stuck
  • Explaining a topic at a few different levels

Where it slips

  • It states wrong facts with full confidence, including doses, ranges, and procedures.
  • It sometimes invents sources, quotes, or references that don’t exist.
  • It doesn’t know your program, your clinic, your students, or your state’s rules unless you tell it.
  • It doesn’t always save time. Checking its work takes time too, and that’s where your expertise comes in.

What stays out

Treat anything you type into a free AI tool like something you’d write on a whiteboard in a busy hallway. Keep these out:

  • Client names, contact details, or account information
  • Patient records that could identify a client or practice
  • Student names, grades, or student work
  • Anything your institution or clinic treats as confidential

Your institution may also have its own rules about which tools you can use. If you’re not sure, it’s worth asking.

If you ever pause before pasting something in, trust that pause. We’ll come back to it on December 1.
